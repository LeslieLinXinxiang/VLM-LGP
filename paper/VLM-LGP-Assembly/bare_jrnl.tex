  \section{Preliminaries and Problem Statement}
  \label{sec:preliminaries}

  Native LGP provides a more integrated formulation of task and motion than
  traditional symbolic planning pipelines, making it attractive for robotic assembly.
  However, prior long-horizon formulations remain difficult to scale in complex
  stacking settings, where the solver may explore many symbolic alternatives before
  geometric infeasibility can effectively prune the search. As a result, directly
  solving long-horizon assemblies in native LGP often incurs high front-end search
  cost and limited practical scalability. To address this limitation, we introduce
  VLM-LGP, which inserts a visual-structural interface before native LGP so that the
  long-horizon task can be reorganized into a form that is more suitable for
  downstream solving.

  We formulate the target structure as an object-support graph and seek a
  precedence-consistent decomposition into solver-facing batches whose scale remains
  manageable for downstream native LGP solving.

  This formulation makes explicit that the central challenge is not whether native LGP
  can express the task, but how to expose only a solver-manageable portion of the
  long-horizon structure at each step. Mixed support patterns, such as bridge-like
  substructures, further complicate the symbolic-geometric search because local support
  changes can affect distant parts of the same full-horizon problem. These observations
  motivate a decomposition stage between support-graph generation and native LGP
  instantiation, so that structural grouping and precedence-respecting execution order
  can be resolved before downstream geometric solving.

  \section{Method}
  \begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Flowchart.pdf}
    \caption{The overall workflow of the proposed VLM-LGP framework.}
    \label{fig:workflow}
  \end{figure*}

  \label{sec:method}

  VLM-LGP addresses long-horizon assembly through a staged pipeline that bridges visual
  task understanding and native LGP solving. At a high level, the framework is organized
  around two parallel streams. Task Decomposition, consisting of VLM Graph Generation,
  Two-Level Clustering, and Global Execution Sequence, converts the target assembly image
  into a solver-facing execution structure. In parallel, Scene Grounding, consisting of
  Reachability Screening and
  Manipulability-Aware Ordering, prepares scene-side feasibility information for the
  available object instances. The two streams are merged at Execution-Order Embedding and
  then passed to Selective LGP Solving with Active Constraints. This organization
  preserves the expressive benefits of native LGP while avoiding a monolithic
  full-horizon formulation. Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarizes the overall workflow,
  and the remainder of this section details each stage.

  \subsection{VLM Graph Generation}
  \label{subsec:phase1}

  We use the VLM to generate an object-support graph from the target assembly image,
  rather than asking it to predict precise object coordinates or a long executable
  program directly. This choice follows the capability boundary of current VLMs: they
  are comparatively reliable at inferring relational structure, support dependencies,
  and coarse left-right or above-below organization, but less stable when required to
  output exact geometry, long symbolic code, or mathematically precise layouts. We
  therefore let the VLM produce the structural representation that it is best suited to
  provide. Formally, the generated graph is $\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})$,
  where each node $v \in \mathcal{V}$ denotes one target object instance and each
  directed edge $(u,v) \in \mathcal{E}$ indicates that object $u$ must support object
  $v$ in the final assembly. The graph is directed because support precedence is
  asymmetric, and optional local labels such as left/right placement hints are retained
  only as geometric side information rather than as the execution order itself.

  Given this structural representation, we follow the general logic of graph
  clustering and partitioning to decompose the long-horizon task into solver-facing
  subproblems~\cite{cite_graph_partition_1998}. In our setting, however, the goal is
  not generic graph partition quality alone. The decomposition must also preserve
  support precedence, respect solver scale, and yield an executable global order for
  downstream native LGP solving. We therefore instantiate this idea as a two-level
  procedure: Branch Clustering first groups structurally related support chains into
  coarse branches, and Layer-Based Cutting then refines each branch into small
  executable batches. The resulting support graph serves as the central interface of
  the pipeline, connecting VLM-based structural parsing to decomposition and selective
  native LGP instantiation. Figure~\ref{fig:layered_graph} shows representative
  support graphs parsed from target assembly images.

  \subsection{Two-Level Clustering}
  \label{subsec:phase2}

  Directly exposing the full support graph to native LGP remains undesirable in long-horizon
  assembly because the resulting symbolic-geometric search is still too coarse and too
  expensive to solve efficiently. We therefore introduce a graph-clustering stage that
  first aggregates the support graph into branch-level structures before any execution
  batches are generated. Among standard graph-clustering paradigms, the closest match to
  our setting is label propagation~\cite{cite_label_propagation_2007}: branch labels are
  seeded at root-supported nodes and then propagated upward along support edges so that
  nodes with the same support provenance fall into the same structural branch, while
  nodes that merge support from multiple roots become bridge branches. In our setting,
  the goal is not generic cluster compactness alone. The branch
  decomposition must also remain consistent with support precedence and preserve the
  coarse structural organization of the target assembly. On the representative example
  used in this paper, this first-stage branch clustering yields the three coarse groups
  $134$, $256$, and $789$.

  Branch-level grouping alone is still insufficient for downstream solving, because even
  after branch decomposition a branch may remain too coarse for the practical solve-time
  budget and hardware limits of the current system. We therefore apply a second
  layer-based cutting stage that follows a simple execution logic inspired by human
  common sense: lower supporting layers are realized before upper ones, left and right
  side structures are expanded in a stable order, and objects on the same effective
  support level are grouped when they can be executed together. This second stage
  converts the coarse branches into solver-facing batches, for example
  $134 \mapsto 1 \mid 34$, $256 \mapsto 2 \mid 56$, and $789 \mapsto 7 \mid 8 \mid 9$.

  \subsubsection{Global Execution Sequence}
  \label{subsec:phase2_global}

  A final precedence-consistent merge over these local batch results produces the global
  execution sequence $1 \mid 34 \mid 2 \mid 56 \mid 7 \mid 8 \mid 9$.

  \subsection{Scene Grounding}
  \label{subsec:scene_grounding}

  Scene Grounding prepares the initial scene for downstream solving by identifying which
  object instances remain executable and how they should be preferred when multiple
  feasible candidates are available. It contains two complementary stages:
  Reachability Screening removes infeasible objects, and Manipulability-Aware Ordering
  ranks the reachable remainder.

  \subsubsection{Reachability Screening}
  \label{subsec:phase0}

  As the first component of Scene Grounding, reachability screening provides the
  scene-side feasibility counterpart to task decomposition. It does not determine the
  structural assembly order; instead, it prunes obviously infeasible objects before
  execution, so that later solver calls are not spent on targets that cannot be safely
  approached under the current scene geometry.

  We implement this stage as a lightweight pick-waypoint feasibility test backed by
  FCL and libccd, allowing each candidate object to be screened through a shallow and
  inexpensive geometric search before full planning. FCL performs broad-phase collision
  pairing, and libccd provides the fine collision and distance evaluation for the
  resulting candidate contacts. For each candidate object $o_i$, we instantiate a
  pick-waypoint subproblem $\Pi_i^{\mathrm{pick}}$ and declare the object reachable
  iff the solver returns a feasible solution:
  \begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Reach}(o_i) =
    \mathbb{I}\!\left[
      \mathrm{Feasible}\!\left(\Pi_i^{\mathrm{pick}}(o_i)\right)
    \right]
    \label{eq:reachability}
  \end{equation}
  Objects that admit a feasible pick waypoint are retained for subsequent execution,
  whereas the remaining candidates are excluded as unreachable. This provides a shallow
  but geometry-consistent feasibility prior at low cost. Although the screening itself
  is lightweight, the same collision-evaluation logic is also compatible with the native
  LGP stack used later in the full pipeline.

  \subsubsection{Manipulability-Aware Ordering}
  \label{subsec:phase0_manip}

  Manipulability-Aware Ordering is applied only to the reachable object set returned by
  reachability screening. Its role is not to alter the structural precedence already
  produced by Task Decomposition, but to provide a scene-side preference over candidate
  instances when multiple reachable objects can satisfy the same structural request.

  In the current implementation, this stage performs a static URDF-based kinematic
  analysis on each reachable candidate. For each object $o_i$, we define an approach
  target slightly above the object, solve a damped least-squares inverse-kinematics
  problem for that target, and evaluate the positional Yoshikawa manipulability
  score~\cite{cite_manipulability_1985}
  \begin{equation}
    m(o_i) = \sqrt{\det\!\left(J_p(q_i)J_p(q_i)^\top\right)},
    \label{eq:manipulability}
  \end{equation}
  where $J_p(q_i)$ denotes the position Jacobian at the corresponding solution
  configuration. The resulting scores are normalized to produce a manipulability-ranked
  candidate list, while low-confidence or IK-invalid cases are deferred to the tail of
  the corresponding object-type group. This stage therefore yields the
  manipulability-ranked candidates used later for scene-side binding.

  \subsection{Execution-Order Embedding}
  \label{subsec:phase_embed}

  Execution-Order Embedding is the stage where the outputs of Task Decomposition and
  Scene Grounding are merged. Task Decomposition provides the global execution sequence
  together with solver-facing support batches, whereas Scene Grounding provides the
  reachable object set and manipulability-ranked candidates for each object category.
  For each batch, the system embeds the structural order into the current scene by
  binding the batch-level object requests to concrete scene instances that survive
  reachability screening and are preferred by manipulability. The output of this stage
  is a grounded batch specification that can be directly consumed by the downstream LGP
  solver.

  \subsection{Selective LGP Solving with Active Constraints}
  \label{subsec:phase3}

  Native LGP couples symbolic task structure with continuous trajectory
  optimization through a shared representation of predicates, action semantics,
  and decision rules~\cite{cite_lgp_2015}. In principle, this abstraction can
  solve assembly tasks from symbolic goals alone. In long-horizon settings,
  however, directly exposing the full generic task domain still leads to
  excessive symbolic branching and unnecessary geometric backtracking.

  Our solver therefore instantiates the native LGP abstraction selectively for
  each grounded execution batch. For the current batch, it compiles only the
  symbolic relations, object bindings, and decision rules required by the active
  support pattern. The solver thus receives a grounded symbolic-geometric
  subproblem tailored to the current batch rather than a monolithic domain
  covering all possible assembly alternatives.

  We first keep the native LGP formulation in its original joint form~\cite{cite_lgp_2015}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{\substack{x,\,a_{1:K},\\ s_{1:K},\,t_{1:K}}}\quad
    &\int_0^T c\!\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t),\ddot{x}(t)\right)\,dt + \psi\!\left(x(T)\right)
    \label{eq:lgp_joint_objective}\\
    \text{s.t.}\quad
    &\forall k=1{:}K\ \ s_k = \mathrm{succ}(a_k, s_{k-1}),
    \label{eq:lgp_logic_transition}\\
    &s_K\models g,
    \label{eq:lgp_goal_constraint}\\
    &\forall_{k=1}^{K}\ h_{\mathrm{switch}}\!\left(x(t_k)\mid a_k,s_{k-1}\right)=0,
    \label{eq:lgp_switch_constraints_eq}\\
    &\forall_{k=1}^{K}\ g_{\mathrm{switch}}\!\left(x(t_k)\mid a_k,s_{k-1}\right)\le 0,
    \label{eq:lgp_switch_constraints_ineq}\\
    &\forall t\in[0,T]\ h_{\mathrm{path}}\!\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t)\mid s_{k(t)}\right)=0,
    \label{eq:lgp_path_constraints_eq}\\
    &\forall t\in[0,T]\ g_{\mathrm{path}}\!\left(x(t),\dot{x}(t)\mid s_{k(t)}\right)\le 0.
    \label{eq:lgp_path_constraints_ineq}
  \end{align}

  For each grounded batch, we then solve the corresponding KOMO subproblem in
  its original constrained sum-of-squares form~\cite{cite_komo_2014}
  \begin{align}
    \min_{x_{0:T}}\quad
    &\sum_{t=0}^{T} f_t\!\left(x_{t-k:t}\right)^\top f_t\!\left(x_{t-k:t}\right)
    +\sum_{t,t'} k(t,t')\,x_t^\top x_{t'}
    \label{eq:komo_batch}\\
    \text{s.t.}\quad
    &\forall t:\ g_t\!\left(x_{t-k:t}\right)\le 0,\ h_t\!\left(x_{t-k:t}\right)=0.
    \label{eq:komo_batch_constraints}
  \end{align}

  The same waypoint-level solve also provides geometric cues for downstream
  motion optimization. Let $x$ denote the full-motion trajectory optimized in
  the downstream stage, let $d_{ij}(x)$ denote the minimum pairwise distance
  between collision proxy frames $f_i$ and $f_j$ along $x$, and let $f_i^w$ denote
  the pose of frame $f_i$ at waypoint $w$. The downstream trajectory is obtained
  by solving
  \begin{equation}
    x^* = \arg\min_x J(x),
    \label{eq:active_objective}
  \end{equation}
  subject to collision constraints instantiated only for the active set
  \begin{equation}
    c_{ij}(x) = d_{\mathrm{safe}} - d_{ij}(x) \leq 0,
    \quad \forall (f_i, f_j) \in \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{active}}(w),
    \label{eq:active_constraints}
  \end{equation}
  where
  \begin{equation}
    \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{active}}(w) =
    \left\{(f_i, f_j)\ \middle|\ d(f_i^w, f_j^w) < r_{\mathrm{active}}\right\}.
    \label{eq:active_set}
  \end{equation}
  Here, $J(x)$ denotes the downstream motion objective, $d_{\mathrm{safe}}$ is
  the prescribed safety margin, and $r_{\mathrm{active}}$ is the activation
  radius used to screen candidate collision pairs from waypoint geometry.
  Only pairs selected by $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{active}}(w)$ are instantiated
  as explicit collision constraints in the downstream full-motion solve. This
  active-constraint strategy reduces unnecessary collision checks and shortens
  full-motion solve time without altering the structural plan itself.

  This restricted instantiation preserves the expressiveness of native LGP while
  concentrating the search on the structurally relevant portion of the task. It
  also provides a clean interface between graph-level decomposition and
  geometric solving: the decomposition module decides which support relations should be
  realized next, and native LGP resolves whether and how those relations can be
  executed under the current scene geometry.

  \subsection{Real Robot Execution}
  \label{subsec:phase4}

  After each subproblem is solved, the resulting trajectory is parsed and
  dispatched to the robot controller for execution on the physical platform.
  The execution layer remains intentionally thin: it reads the planned motion
  and gripper commands, executes them on the real robot, and updates the scene
  state for the next planning-solving cycle.
